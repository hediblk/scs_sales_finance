Option B — Sales & Finance · Chrome & Oak Office
Supply
Who they are

Chrome & Oak Office Supply is a Montreal-based company, founded in 2000, that sells office
and home-office equipment (desks, chairs, monitors and accessories) across Canada. It sells
to individual consumers through its website, to small businesses and large companies through
account reps, and to resellers who sell Chrome & Oak products to their own clients.

How the business works
       •    Every product has a list price. Account reps can give discounts to win or keep a
            deal. Each rep has a quarterly revenue target, and hitting it earns a bonus.
       •    Consumers pay at checkout. Business customers pay later, on terms: “Net 30”
            means the invoice is due 30 days after the order, “Net 60” means 60 days.
       •    Each business customer has a credit limit: the maximum amount it’s supposed to
            owe Chrome & Oak at any time.
       •    Customers can return products. Chrome & Oak refunds them, and damaged items
            usually can’t be resold.

What’s happening

On paper, Chrome & Oak has never looked better. But the leadership team is uneasy:
    CEO: “Revenue is up about a third compared to last year. Our Atlantic team punches
    way above its weight. Let’s keep doing what we’re doing.”
    CFO: “Then why is cash so tight? Revenue is growing but profit isn’t keeping up, and
    I’m chasing unpaid invoices every week.”
    Operations Manager: “Returns are eating my warehouse team’s time, especially the
    big items.”
    Head of Customer Success: “Some of our long-time business customers have gone
    quiet. Nobody told me why.”

Your role

You’ve been brought in as a small analytics and tools team. Find out what’s really driving the
gap between how good things look and how they actually are, decide which problem is
worth solving first, and build a tool Chrome & Oak’s team could use to act on it.

Your data: customers.csv, orders.csv and returns.csv, covering orders from January 2025 to
September 2026, extracted on October 31, 2026.



The Dataset
The selected domain comes with:
       •    the short business scenario above
       •    the data files exported from the company’s systems

There is no data dictionary. Figuring out what each file and column represents, and how the
files connect to each other, is part of the challenge.

The data came straight from internal systems. Don’t assume it’s clean.

The purpose of the dataset is not to test business knowledge. It is to test your ability to reason
about data, communicate your findings, and learn while getting hands-on experience.



How to Approach It
Work through these stages in order. In practice you’ll go back and forth between them, and
that’s normal. At the end of each stage, there’s a short note on what you should be able to
show.


1. Understand the business
Read the scenario carefully before touching any code. Ask yourselves:
       •    What does this company do, and how does it make money?
       •    What are the people in the scenario worried about? Do they agree with each other?
       •    What would “a problem worth solving” look like for this company?

Don’t assume you already know what the problem is. The people in the scenario each have an
opinion. Your data will tell you who is right.

Show: You understand how the business works and why the problem you chose matters to it.


2. Ask questions and explore the data
Start by understanding the data itself: what each file contains, what each row represents, and
how the files relate to each other. Then start asking business questions.

Good questions combine more than one piece of information. For example:

Instead of:
     “Which product sells the most?”

A stronger question could be:
     “Are our highest-revenue products actually our most profitable products?”

This requires you to combine multiple pieces of information and draw a more meaningful
conclusion. The strongest projects come up with their own questions and show independent
reasoning, rather than stopping at the first obvious chart.

Show: The questions you asked, and why you thought they were worth asking.


3. Prepare the data
Before you can trust any number, you need to make the data usable. This might mean cleaning,
filtering, fixing types, combining files, grouping, aggregating, or creating new calculated
columns.

You do not need to explain every line of code. What you do need to explain is the reasoning
behind your changes. For example, if you group transactions by category, remove duplicates, or
create a new column, you should be able to explain:
       •      Why did you make this change?
       •      What were you trying to understand?
       •      Did it change any of your conclusions?

Small cleaning decisions can completely change what the data seems to say. Pay attention to
them.

Show: What you changed in the data, why, and how it affected what you found.


4. Find insights and visualize them
Analyze the prepared data to find what’s really going on. Useful tools include totals, averages,
percentages, growth rates, comparisons between groups, rankings, trends and correlations.

Insights. Identify at least 2 meaningful insights. Each insight should explain:
       •      What happened?
       •      Why does it matter?
       •      What should the business consider doing?

At least one of your insights must be the reason behind your automation (see stage 6).

Visualizations. Create at least 2 meaningful visualizations that help explain your insights.
They can live inside your prototype (for example, as part of a dashboard), and that’s
encouraged.

Examples: bar charts, line charts, scatter plots, histograms, heatmaps, pie/donut charts.

Think carefully about which visualizations you choose and how relevant they are to your
solution. A good chart helps someone understand a business situation and make a decision. It
doesn’t just display data.

Show: Your insights, the evidence behind them, and visuals that make them easy to
understand.


5. Build your prototype
Translate what you found into a working software prototype that addresses the problem you
identified.

There are no restrictions on the type of solution. It could be:
       •    a web application
       •    a software or desktop application
       •    a dashboard with interactive functionality
       •    an internal business tool
       •    an operational management or decision-support system
       •    another creative software solution

A command-line interface on its own does not count as a prototype for this track.

Your prototype must use the provided dataset, and it should be something the company’s staff
could actually picture themselves using.

Show: A working prototype that clearly connects to the problem you identified.


6. Automate
Your prototype must:
       •    Detect at least 2 meaningful business conditions in the data.
       •    Automatically trigger at least 1 action when one of those conditions occurs.
       •    Be justified by your analysis: your automation should exist because of something
            you found in the data, not just to satisfy a requirement.

What is an automation? An automation checks a condition in the data and does something
useful without a person having to do it manually.

Here’s an example from a different business, so you can see the shape of it:
     A gym member who used to come 3 times a week hasn’t checked in for 30 days
     → System flags the member as “at risk” and sends a win-back offer.

How you design your automation is up to you, and it’s one of the most important parts of the
challenge. As you design yours, ask yourselves:
       •    Who receives the result?
       •    What do they do next?
       •    What happens if your automation is wrong?

Show: What your system detects, what happens when it does, and why that’s useful for the
business.



Minimum Requirements
By the end of the build, your team must have:
       1.   A clear business problem you identified from the scenario and the data.
       2.   Prepared data, with reasoning behind your main transformations.
       3.   At least 2 meaningful insights (What happened? Why does it matter? What should
            the business consider doing?).
       4.   At least 2 meaningful visualizations.
       5.   A working prototype (not just a command-line interface).
       6.   At least 2 detected business conditions and at least 1 automated action,
            justified by your insights.



AI
AI tools ARE allowed.

Teams may use tools such as ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, Claude, Gemini, Cursor, or AI APIs, for
example to:
       •    assist with coding and debugging
       •    understand unfamiliar libraries and frameworks
       •    explore ideas and possible approaches
       •    classify or summarize information
       •    generate recommendations or natural-language explanations
       •    enhance your workflow

However:
       •    AI cannot be the entire solution. For example, simply sending the dataset to an AI
            model and displaying its response does not count as a strong automation system.
       •    At least one of your detections must be logic your team wrote and can
            explain. AI can summarize, explain or recommend on top of it.

       •    You must understand what you build. AI can help you analyze the data,
            challenge your assumptions, or suggest alternatives. But you should be the ones
            deciding what to investigate, whether an insight is actually supported by the data,
            and how your findings shape your solution.

We strongly encourage you to do the business reasoning yourselves. Understanding the
business, identifying the problem, choosing your questions, and judging whether an insight is
meaningful are exactly the skills this track is designed to build. Use AI as a tool, not as a
replacement for your thinking. The judges will ask you about your reasoning, not about whether
you used AI.



Recommended Tools
Teams may use any programming language or framework.

That said, we recommend Python. It’s one of the most widely used languages for working with
data, and its libraries for data analysis, visualization and machine learning are among the best
available.

Hint: this challenge uses structured, table-shaped data. Some Python libraries were built for
exactly that kind of data, and knowing them will save you a lot of time.



Optional Advanced Features
Teams can go beyond the minimum requirements by adding:


Simulation
Allow the user to change a value and see what would happen.

Example: “What happens if we change this threshold?”


Multiple-step Automation
One detection triggers a chain of actions.

Example: Condition detected → identify who is affected → recommend an action → notify the
right person → log what happened.


AI-Assisted Decision Making
Example: System detects an unusual case → AI summarizes the relevant information → system
recommends whether a human should review it.

Interactive Exploration
Let the user explore the data behind an alert, for example by filtering, drilling down, or
comparing groups, to understand why the automation was triggered.



Presentation
Each team will have a 5-minute presentation and demo, followed by 2–3 minutes of
questions from the judges.

No slides are needed. Use your prototype to tell the story.

Suggested time split:

 Part                                                                                Time

 The business problem and why it matters                                             ~1 min

 What you found in the data (your insights and visualizations)                       ~1.5 min

 Live demo of your prototype, with your automation in action                         ~2 min

 Your recommendation to the business                                                 ~30 sec

You should be ready to explain:
        •   Which domain you selected and what business problem you identified.
        •   How you processed the data, and why.
        •   What you discovered and why you chose your visualizations.
        •   What your system detects, what happens when it does, and why that action is
            useful.
        •   What decision or recommendation you would give the business.

You don’t need to walk through your code line by line. But you should understand what you built
and be ready to explain the reasoning behind your data transformations, your automation and
every part of your solution. Expect questions. The Q&A is where we find out how well you
understand your work.



Submission
There is no formal submission at this time. Your presentation and live demo are what will be
evaluated. If a submission step is added, we will announce it before the event.

Rules
       •   Teams consist of 2 participants.
       •   Development time: 4 hours.
       •   Teams may use any programming language or framework.
       •   AI tools are permitted (see the AI section).
       •   Official documentation, tutorials, Stack Overflow and other programming resources
           are permitted.
       •   External APIs are permitted, provided they are accessible during the competition.
       •   All major implementation work must take place during the competition. Starter
           templates and libraries are fine. Project code written before the event is not.
       •   Teams must be able to explain their work to the judges.



Important
There is no single correct answer.

Two teams may analyze the same dataset and reach completely different conclusions. We are
interested in how you use the data to support your reasoning.

The goal is not to automate everything. Build a focused solution that solves one business
problem well. A simple automation that reliably solves a real problem is better than a
complicated system with many unfinished features.

               Good luck and enjoy your time learning and getting challenged! :D
